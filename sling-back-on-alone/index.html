Getting Your Arm Back in the Sling Solo: What to Know

By James Park · October 2026

Getting your arm back in the sling alone doesn't have to be a wrestling match — one seated method, published by North Bristol NHS Trust, breaks it into a fixed sequence. You sit down, rest the forearm on a pillow, slide the trough in from the side, then fasten the straps forearm first, wrist second, shoulder third, and waist last if it's prescribed.

Can you put a shoulder sling back on without help?

Yes — North Bristol NHS Trust publishes a version of the refit meant for exactly this. Instead of a helper sliding the trough in from behind, you sit, bend the elbow to roughly 90 degrees, rest the forearm on a pillow, and slide the trough in from the side with your free hand. The motion changes, but the goal is the same: the forearm stays supported before the straps come into play.

Why does the sling need to come off and go back on so often?

Because most rehab schedules build removal in, not just for showers. One published protocol calls for taking the sling off 4 to 5 times a day for elbow, wrist, and finger exercises during the early weeks, plus once for bathing. Over a six-week sling period, that can mean roughly 210 to 250 solo refits. Other protocols schedule fewer sessions, but frequent short removals are the norm across them.

Tip

A firm, elbow-height surface — a counter edge, a table, or a dense pillow on the lap — can substitute for a helper by keeping the forearm supported while your free hand manages the straps.

What should be ready before you start?

Mainly the straps, the sling's orientation, and a support surface. Velcro any loose straps back onto themselves so they don't tangle mid-motion, confirm the wrist strap's D-rings are facing the right way, and have a pillow or firm surface at elbow height within reach. Getting dressed first, then fitting the sling over clothing, tends to go smoother than the reverse.

What's the actual motion for sliding the sling on?

Sit down with the elbow near 90 degrees and the forearm resting on a pillow, then slide the trough in from the side until the elbow sits snugly in its corner. That seated position is the base everything else locks around — once the elbow is settled, the straps hold it rather than you having to.

In what order do the straps get fastened?

Forearm strap first, about an inch below the elbow crease, then the wrist strap about an inch from the wrist. The shoulder strap crosses the back, goes over the unoperated shoulder, and threads through the upper D-ring before Velcroing onto itself. A waist strap, if your setup includes one, goes through the lower D-ring last. The finished arm should sit close to the body, knuckles forward, wrist level with or slightly above the elbow — a position tied to less hand swelling in the North Bristol instructions.

Watch out

The operated arm shouldn't do any lifting or holding during this process — using it to help can move the shoulder more than intended and may put the repair at risk.

Does an abduction pillow sling refit the same way?

No — the bulkier abduction immobilizer, which holds a pillow between arm and torso, follows a different order per Oklahoma Shoulder Center: lean toward the operated side to guide the arm in, fasten the forearm strap, then buckle the waist strap before the neck strap. That sequence keeps the pillow from shifting mid-refit. The wedge shape isn't incidental — it's meant to hold close to the 30-degree scapular-plane elevation some protocols describe as a safer resting angle, which is part of why a wedge like the Aeris Wedge Pillow shows up for support at night, too.

What tends to go wrong when refitting alone?

Standing up is the most common mistake, since it leaves nothing supporting the forearm while your free hand works the straps. Letting the elbow slip out of the trough's corner before fastening is another — the arm ends up misaligned no matter how snug the straps feel after. Loose or tangled straps turn a short routine into a search, and reaching with the operated arm to speed things up works against the reason you're wearing the sling at all.

When should you stop troubleshooting and call the clinic?

If the sling won't seat properly after a careful, seated attempt, or if you notice numbness, tingling, color change, climbing pain, or redness under a strap that doesn't fade, it's time to call your surgeon's office rather than keep adjusting alone. Protocols also disagree on details like sling duration and removal frequency, so where your own discharge sheet differs from anything above, follow your sheet.

Frequently asked questions

Can you refit a shoulder sling without someone helping you?

Yes — North Bristol NHS Trust describes a solo method: sit down, bend the elbow to about 90 degrees, rest the forearm on a pillow, and slide the trough in from the side rather than from behind. The straps then fasten one at a time with your free hand. Whether you're cleared to go without the sling entirely is a separate question for your surgeon.

What order do the sling straps go in?

Forearm strap first, roughly an inch below the elbow crease, then the wrist strap about an inch from the wrist. The shoulder strap crosses the back and threads through the upper D-ring, and the waist strap, if used, goes through the lower D-ring last. This order can help keep the arm from shifting while each piece gets fastened.

How many times a day does the sling typically come off?

It depends on the protocol you've been given. One published protocol calls for 4 to 5 removals a day for elbow and wrist exercises plus showering, while others schedule around three sessions a day. Over six weeks, that can add up to well over 200 refittings, so a consistent routine matters.

How do you know the sling is positioned correctly?

Check the finished shape rather than how tight it feels. The arm should sit close to the body, knuckles facing forward, elbow snug in the trough's corner, with the wrist level with or slightly above the elbow — a position linked to less hand swelling in published instructions.

Is an abduction pillow sling refit differently from a standard one?

Yes — it generally involves leaning toward the operated side to guide the arm in, then buckling the waist strap before the neck strap so the pillow doesn't shift. The pillow holds a resting angle some protocols describe as safer for a healing repair, so working with it tends to go better than forcing it aside.
