Wash Your Hair After Shoulder Surgery: A Quick Q&A

By James Park · October 2026

Most people can wash their hair within the first week after shoulder surgery, as long as the operated arm stays passive and any wound dressing is kept intact. The method changes — a forward lean over a basin or shower stands in for a normal overhead wash — but the task itself isn't off-limits. Below are the questions patients ask most, answered with what published patient-information leaflets and surgical guidance actually say.

Is it safe to wash your hair after shoulder surgery?

Yes — in most post-op protocols, hair washing is treated as routine hygiene rather than something to delay. North Bristol NHS Trust tells patients they "may wash your hair in the shower or by leaning over a sink or bath," with one condition attached to every version of the advice: the operated arm does not help. If reaching for a bottle or bracing on that side feels necessary, the position needs adjusting rather than pushing through it.

When can you actually get your head wet?

The limiting factor is usually the wound dressing, not the joint. University Hospital Southampton NHS Foundation Trust notes that keyhole rotator cuff repairs are typically closed with adhesive strips under "waterproof dressings... so you can wash and shower," though a dressing that's soaked through needs changing. Open procedures and individual surgeons' preferences vary, so the timeline on your own discharge sheet overrides any general number.

Note

A dressing that's fully soaked should be changed rather than left to dry on its own — check your discharge paperwork for who to contact if that happens.

Does it matter whether the sling is on or off?

Yes, and this is the detail most general advice skips. North Bristol NHS Trust publishes two separate routines: patients cleared to remove the sling can shower or lean over a basin with the arm hanging free, while patients told to keep the sling on at all times lean forward with the arm still inside it, taking care to keep the sling itself dry. Either way, the operated arm stays uninvolved in the actual washing.

What should you avoid doing while washing your hair?

The main risks are using the operated arm, lifting it away from the body, or pushing on it for balance. North Bristol NHS Trust warns that using the operated side to help "may cause damage to the surgery," so the wash position should let that arm hang or rest rather than brace against a sink or bath edge. A non-slip mat and a seated position can reduce the chance of an off-balance reach.

When can you go back to washing with both hands?

Not for a while, according to published occupational-therapy guidance. A patient guide written by Robyn Fitzpatrick for Dr Simon Tan, Living with a sling after surgery, places two-handed shampooing in a 6-to-12-week window. Before that, around weeks 4 to 6, it expects patients to manage a hairdryer or brush with the operated hand while supporting the elbow — brushing only the far side of the head. Full two-arm shampooing comes later than either of those milestones.

Why does washing your hair take so much shoulder movement?

Because reaching the crown of your own head is a genuinely demanding motion. Researchers who tracked the ten functional tasks used in standard shoulder outcome scores found they required an average of 121° of flexion and 128° of abduction — angles well above what an early recovery protocol typically allows. Leaning the head forward and down substitutes trunk movement for shoulder movement, which is why the basin method works when reaching overhead doesn't.

Tip

Dry shampoo isn't a long-term fix, but it's named by more than one source as a reasonable way to stretch the gap between proper washes, especially in the first couple of weeks.

What can you do on the days you skip a wash?

Spacing out washes is a legitimate option, not a shortcut. Canterbury Upper Limb and North Bristol NHS Trust both mention dry shampoo as a short-term bridge, and a shower cap can keep hair dry during an otherwise necessary shower. Styles that don't require overhead arm movement, or a salon visit where someone else does the reaching, are also reasonable while the shoulder catches up. Persistent pain, swelling, or a wound that won't stop weeping is a reason to call your surgical team rather than wait it out.

What helps with the daytime one-handed routine — a seat, pump-bottle shampoo, a handheld shower head — tends to overlap with what helps overnight, since most people spend the first weeks recovering in a reclined position. Frequently asked questions

How soon after shoulder surgery can I wash my hair?

Often within the first few days, since many keyhole repairs use waterproof dressings specifically so showering can continue. University Hospital Southampton NHS describes these dressings as being there "so you can wash and shower." Exact timing still depends on your surgeon's instructions and whether the repair was open or arthroscopic.

Can I wash my hair while the sling is still on?

Yes, if that's what you've been told to do. North Bristol NHS Trust describes leaning over a basin, bath, or shower with the arm kept inside the sling, taking care not to soak the sling itself and avoiding any use of the operated arm to help.

When will I be able to shampoo normally with both hands?

Published occupational-therapy guidance places that milestone at roughly 6 to 12 weeks after surgery, following an intermediate stage around weeks 4 to 6 where you can hold a hairdryer or brush with support. Individual recovery speeds vary, so treat this as a general expectation rather than a fixed date.

Do I need help washing my hair at first?

Many people do, and it's considered normal rather than a setback. North Bristol NHS Trust notes some patients need assistance washing their hair in the early weeks, and leaning over a basin with a helper nearby is often easier than attempting it alone.

Will washing my hair hurt my repair?

Not if the operated arm stays passive throughout. The consistent condition across patient guidance is that the unoperated hand does all the work; it's using or lifting the operated arm — not washing itself — that carries the risk.
