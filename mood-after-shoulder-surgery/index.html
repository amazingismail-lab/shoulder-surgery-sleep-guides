Depression After Shoulder Surgery: Is It Normal?

By James Park · October 2026

A dip in mood after shoulder surgery shows up often enough in the research that it's worth understanding ahead of time rather than being surprised by it. The clearest data comes from a 2026 study of 42 rotator cuff repair patients, whose depression and anxiety scores rose for months before settling back down — and from a few other studies that complicate the simple version of that story.

Is depression after shoulder surgery a normal part of recovery?

It can be, and it's common enough to have been measured in several cohorts. In the 2026 JSES International study, patients scored themselves on the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) nine times across a year, and scores climbed well above baseline before falling back. It's worth noting these are symptom questionnaires, not clinical diagnoses, and depression among people with rotator cuff tears was already running at 12.8% to 26.2% before anyone had surgery.

Tip

Scoring high on a mood questionnaire isn't the same as having a diagnosed depressive disorder — most of the figures below come from self-reported scales, not psychiatric evaluation.

When does the low mood tend to peak?

In that 42-patient group, the worst point was three months out. Depression scores went from 3.1 before surgery to 7.8 at six weeks, peaked at 10.7 at three months, then dropped to 2.7 at nine months and 1.6 at twelve — ending up roughly half the starting value. Anxiety followed almost the same curve. Three months is also, awkwardly, around when the shoulder itself often looks fine on imaging and exams, even though it doesn't feel that way yet.

Why did a separate study find the opposite pattern?

Because the two groups started in different places. A 2023 study of 43 cuff repair patients found mood scores falling steadily from the first month onward, not rising. That group had worse pre-surgery shoulder function and higher starting depression scores (5.0 versus 3.1), so for them, surgery mostly brought relief rather than a new low point. The takeaway isn't "will my mood drop" so much as "what was my baseline going in" — patients worn down by pain beforehand may feel better quickly, while those who went in relatively well may be the ones surprised at month three.

What's actually driving the dip?

No single cause has been isolated, but four things tend to move together: pain that worsens at night, broken sleep, weeks of one-handed dependence on others for basic tasks, and a growing gap between how recovery was expected to feel and how it actually feels. In the JSES cohort, the link between poor shoulder function and low mood was strongest at exactly three months — a correlation of −0.79 — which lines up with when the sling comes off but the arm still isn't reliable.

Does bad sleep cause the mood dip, or the other way around?

The one study that tested this directly found depression scores predicting poor sleep, not the reverse. In a 2024 study of 133 patients six months post-repair, the group with poor sleep also had notably higher depression and anxiety scores, and in a combined statistical model, depression independently predicted later poor sleep with an odds ratio of 7.21 — while pain and anxiety dropped out as predictors. The confidence interval was wide, so treat the exact number loosely, but the direction suggests sleep fixes alone may not be enough on their own. Does shoulder replacement carry the same risk?

It appears to, at a smaller measured rate. A claims-database study of 144,773 shoulder replacement patients with no prior psychiatric history found 3.0% received a new depression diagnosis within a year. That's a floor, not a full picture, since it only counts people who sought and received a diagnosis — and the study had no non-surgical comparison group, so it can't say surgery caused the depression. The same group had higher rates of 90-day complications and readmission, though which came first isn't established.

Watch out

If low mood has lasted more than two weeks without improving, or seems to be getting worse rather than better as the shoulder heals, that's a reason to contact your surgeon's office or your own doctor. If you're having thoughts of self-harm, treat it as urgent — in the US, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline answers calls and texts 24 hours a day.

What can help in the meantime?

Nothing here treats mood directly, but removing physical causes of nighttime waking is worth doing regardless. Keeping the torso propped rather than flat, supporting the elbow so the arm isn't hanging unsupported, and keeping medication timing consistent through the night can all reduce a specific reason for waking. Some patients use a shaped support like the Flexicomfort Shoulder Pillow to keep the arm from drifting, though no study has tested whether that changes mood outcomes. Frequently asked questions

Is it normal to feel emotional after shoulder surgery?

It can be. Mood questionnaire scores in several studies ran several times higher in the weeks after surgery than before it, so a stronger emotional response during that window is consistent with the measured pattern, even though it isn't guaranteed for everyone.

How long does the mood dip usually last?

In one 42-patient cohort, scores peaked at three months and fell below pre-surgery levels by nine months. That's a single study with one surgeon, so it describes a general shape rather than a fixed timeline anyone should expect to match exactly.

Does a low mood after surgery mean the surgery didn't work?

Not necessarily. In the same cohort, mood scores were worst at three months while shoulder function was still steadily improving. The dip may reflect the gap between expected and actual progress as much as anything about the surgical outcome itself.

Will improving my sleep fix my mood?

It may help, but no study here tested that directly, and the regression analysis available points the other direction — depression predicted poor sleep, not the reverse. Better sleep is worth pursuing on its own merits, though it shouldn't replace talking to a clinician about mood.

Should I mention mood changes to my surgeon?

Yes. Researchers in more than one of these studies recommend early recognition and support, with one noting that symptoms appear correctable with proper assessment. Surgical teams see this often enough that raising it isn't unusual or out of place.
