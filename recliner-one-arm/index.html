Recliner With One Arm After Shoulder Surgery: A Guide

By James Park · November 2026

A recliner with one arm after shoulder surgery is workable for many people, but only if the chair reclines and comes back upright without any push, pull, or weight-bearing from the operated side. A side lever on the surgical side usually fails that test; a power button or remote on the other side usually passes it. This guide walks through what hospital leaflets say about chairs, which recliner mechanisms need two hands, and how to check your own chair before surgery day. Can you work a recliner with one arm after shoulder surgery?

Often, yes, but it depends on the mechanism rather than the brand. Hospital guidance generally asks patients to avoid pushing or pulling with the operated arm while a chair reclines, lowers its footrest, or helps them stand, so a chair only qualifies if those motions can be done with the other arm and the legs alone.

What do hospital leaflets say about pushing up from a chair?

They are consistent on the restriction even when the wording differs. UW Medicine's occupational therapy handout tells patients not to use the surgical arm "to push up from a chair or bed," and separately notes that a recliner "may be the most comfortable" position for sleeping. United Lincolnshire Hospitals is more specific for a moderate cuff repair, naming 6 weeks before pushing up from the arm of a chair is allowed again. The Royal Orthopaedic Hospital states plainly that "no weight must be placed through your operated shoulder whilst getting on/off furniture."

Note

These time frames come from general patient leaflets, not your own chart. Your surgeon's discharge instructions set the actual restriction and when it ends.

Which recliner mechanisms need two arms to operate?

A manual recliner with a side lever typically needs a pull from whichever hand sits next to it, while a push-back manual recliner reclines with body weight and comes forward by pressing the footrest down with the legs. Furniture Academy describes power recliners as using a motor triggered by "a button on the side panel or a wireless remote," which removes the pulling motion entirely. In practice, a power recliner with controls on the non-operated side, or on a remote, tends to be the easiest one-arm option; a push-back chair can also work, but only if you resist the habit of pressing on both armrests to come forward.

What if the lever sits on your operated side?

Then that particular recliner does not count as usable one-armed. Reaching across your body to pull a far-side lever twists the trunk and drags the sling arm along with it, so it is not a real workaround. The practical fixes are a different chair with the lever on the other side, a power recliner with a remote, or building a reclined position on a flat bed instead.

How do you get out of a recliner with one arm?

Mostly with your legs, using the non-operated arm only to steady yourself. The Sky Lakes Medical Center surgery guide describes scooting to the edge of the seat "using your hips and legs," planting the feet, leaning forward at the hips, and then pushing up with the legs to stand, "using your unoperated arm on the armrest to help balance and guide you." In a recliner, the footrest and back need to be level first, which means the button, the lever under the good hand, or pressing the footrest down with the calves.

What happens to a power recliner during a power cut?

A standard power recliner stays put until the power returns, unless it has a battery backup. That is a real concern after shoulder surgery, since forcing yourself out of a chair that is stuck fully reclined is exactly the arm-heavy move the restrictions are trying to prevent. Style Meets Comfort, a La-Z-Boy retailer, notes that rechargeable battery packs work with La-Z-Boy's power furniture "except power lift recliners," meaning a lift chair still depends on mains power. Check in advance whether your chair has a battery option, and keep a flashlight nearby just in case.

How can you test a recliner before surgery day?

By rehearsing the whole sequence one-handed before you actually need it. Recline the chair fully, bring the footrest back down, and stand up, all using only the non-operated arm, then repeat the same sequence with the lights off, since nighttime trips are the real test.

Tip

Wear your sling, or tuck the operated hand into a waistband, during this test so you can't cheat and use it without noticing.

What if no recliner passes the one-arm test?

Then a reclined position built on a flat bed is a reasonable substitute. A foam wedge has no lever, button, or motor to operate, and UW Medicine's own sleeping guidance starts from a flat bed with pillows "under your elbow and hand, but not under your shoulder." Some patients also use a shaped support such as the Flexicomfort Shoulder Pillow to keep the operated arm positioned in either a chair or a bed setup. Frequently asked questions

Can I use a manual recliner after shoulder surgery?

Yes, if the lever is under your non-operated hand and you can bring the chair back upright without involving the operated arm. The restriction in the leaflets cited above is about pushing up from a chair with the surgical arm, not about manual recliners specifically, so a correctly oriented lever can still work.

Is a power recliner easier to use after shoulder surgery?

Usually, since a button or remote replaces the pulling motion of a lever. Make sure the controls sit on your non-operated side or come on a separate remote, and have a backup plan in case of a power outage.

How long do I need to avoid pushing up from a chair?

That depends on your procedure and surgeon. United Lincolnshire Hospitals lists 6 weeks for a moderate cuff repair, NHS Lothian places the restriction in a weeks 4-6 window, and UW Medicine ties it loosely to the sling-wearing period. Always check your own discharge instructions first.

Should I sleep in a recliner or in bed after shoulder surgery?

Either may work, since UW Medicine's handout lists both lying on your back and sleeping in a recliner as positions that may be comfortable. A recliner suits people who can operate it one-armed; a flat bed with a wedge suits people whose recliner fails that test.

What if I have ongoing pain or can't manage the one-arm test safely?

If pain, weakness, or balance problems are making it difficult or unsafe to get in or out of a chair on your own, it's worth checking in with your surgeon or physical therapist rather than continuing to push through it.
